UAV Swarm Spoofing Defense

StatusActive · two preprints on arXiv SettingSimulation — ArduPilot SITL, Gazebo, and real GPS/Galileo orbits OutputRigid-Covert GNSS Spoofing of UAV Swarms · Detecting Formation-Preserving GNSS Spoofing of UAV Swarms

The blind spot

Cooperative swarm defenses usually work by cross-checking what GNSS reports against the geometry the drones can measure among themselves. If a reported position drifts away from where the inter-drone ranges say a drone should be, something is wrong.

That check has a structural hole. If an attacker moves the entire formation slowly and together, every pairwise distance stays exactly as it was. The discrepancy the defense looks for is zero — not small, not below threshold, but zero by construction. No amount of tuning closes it, because it is a gauge freedom rather than a detection failure.

Restoring the frame

What relative measurements can never supply is an absolute frame. Inter-drone ranges determine the swarm's shape perfectly well; they say nothing about where that shape sits in the world. So the recovery works in two halves: rebuild the shape from ranges, then root that shape on a small number of references that do not come from GNSS.

The alignment step has to survive a lying reference, so it is fitted robustly rather than by least squares — a compromised anchor is outvoted instead of averaged in. Once the frame is recovered, absolute positions follow for every drone, including the ones carrying no anchor of their own.

Where it stops working

The interesting part of this problem is not that a defense exists, but that its limits can be stated precisely. There is a drift-dependent floor below which a slow enough attack is indistinguishable from ordinary anchor drift. Anchor placement matters: collinear references leave the frame underdetermined. And if a majority of anchors are compromised, the trust root itself is gone and nothing downstream can repair it.

Every result here is simulation-based. No RF spoofing hardware and no physical swarm were involved.

When the shift is only approximate

An exact common shift is the limiting case, and no real attacker can produce one: it would take transmitters at satellite distance. GNSS-WASP, the realizable version, puts one transmitter per satellite a few kilometres away and keeps the formation only approximately. So the next question was whether that approximation leaves a trace below the position level, in the measurements the positions are computed from.

It does. A satellite is about 20,000 km away, so every drone in a 90 m swarm sees it under practically the same angle. A transmitter 5 km away is seen under directions that differ by about a degree across the same swarm. That parallax bends the signal's arrival times across the formation into a bowl, about 20 cm deep at the edge, and because the attacker's delay for each transmitter is added equally to every drone, no timing can flatten it.

The detector is a likelihood ratio test that reads this bowl from the pseudoranges and Doppler every drone already measures, with every receiver clock and attacker delay left unknown and no extra antennas. Doppler adds evidence when the swarm turns in formation, because the drones then move at different velocities. In simulation with real GPS and Galileo orbits, 16 drones catch GNSS-WASP at 5 km in 13–60% of 10 s windows in straight flight and in every window during a turn with a precise-Doppler receiver, where the countermeasure proposed with GNSS-WASP catches at most 5% on the same swarm.

The attacker's remaining escape is distance, and the distance it needs grows with the square of the swarm's width. The two pieces of work fit together: parallax tells the swarm that a source is near, using only the receivers and inter-drone ranging it already has; anchors are what recover the true position, and what handle an exact shift. This part is also simulation only. The next step is cabled validation, with no over-the-air transmission.
