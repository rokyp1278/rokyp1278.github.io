Ship Tracking and Maritime Boundary Intrusion Early-Warning System Using SAR Satellite Imagery

Why radar, and why sequences

This work comes out of night coastal-guard duty at Yeonpyeong Island. The watch there depends on optical sensors, and those sensors fail exactly when the watch matters most — sea fog, foul weather, and darkness all blind them, and a vessel approaching the Northern Limit Line under those conditions is the case the equipment handles worst. Synthetic aperture radar does not care about fog or daylight.

What changed the question is persistence. SAR satellites have historically flown in low Earth orbit, which limits how often the same water can be revisited, so SAR ship detection has largely been posed as a single-frame problem: where are the vessels in this scene. Geostationary SAR demonstrations have since shown that a fixed patch of sea can be observed continuously, which moves the useful question from where is the ship to where is it going, and will it cross the line.

That second question needs sequences, and public SAR ship datasets do not have them. HRSID provides individual scenes with ship annotations, which is enough to train a detector and not enough to evaluate tracking or alerting at all.

Building the data that does not exist

No public dataset contains SAR sequences of vessels crossing a maritime boundary, so the approach tracks had to be synthesized. The pipeline starts from HRSID and works in three steps:

  • Cut out — ship instances are extracted along their COCO segmentation polygons, so each vessel comes away with its true SAR signature rather than a rectangular crop.
  • Inpaint — the sea left behind is filled in, removing the vessel from its original position without leaving a visible hole.
  • Re-composite — the extracted vessels are placed back along linear approach paths toward a drawn boundary, producing a sequence in which each ship advances frame by frame.

The detector is trained on synthesized sequences, and the evaluation set is built from HRSID scenes that share no source imagery with the training data — 525 held-out sequences in total. Keeping the two sides disjoint is what makes the reported numbers mean something: a detector evaluated on recompositions of its own training scenes would mostly be scoring its own memory.

Detection, tracking, and alerting

Detection uses YOLOv8n-OBB, which returns an oriented bounding box rather than an axis-aligned one. That orientation is what makes the rest possible: a rectangle tells you a ship is present, but an oriented box tells you which way its bow is pointing.

Detections are then linked across frames into tracks, and each track is read for approach angle and range to the boundary. From those, the system predicts whether and when the track would cross, and maps the result to a five-level alert — so a vessel running parallel to the boundary is not treated the same as one bearing down on it.

Results

  • Detection rate — 100.0%
  • Mean IoU — 0.787
  • Alert recall — 84.2%
  • False-alarm rate — 6.9%

Recall and false-alarm rate are reported separately rather than folded into one score, because in a coastal watch the two errors do not cost the same thing.

Summary

  • Motivation — optical watch blinded by fog, weather, and darkness; geostationary SAR makes persistent observation of one patch of sea plausible
  • Gap — public SAR ship datasets are single-frame, so tracking and alerting cannot be evaluated
  • Data — synthetic approach sequences from HRSID via polygon cut-out, inpainting, and re-compositing; 525 held-out evaluation sequences
  • Method — YOLOv8n-OBB detection, cross-frame tracking, approach-angle and crossing prediction, five-level alert
  • Result — 100.0% detection, 0.787 mean IoU, 84.2% alert recall, 6.9% false alarms
